01 · SEE THE WHOLE PICTURE

Our relationship map

Notice what is hard, what is growing, and what you already value in each other and yourselves.

Acceptance & appreciation

Write from your own experience. Acceptance can include recognizing differences while choosing what matters to you.

Similarities & differences

Name one way you are alike and one way you differ. Difference does not have to mean a flaw.

Other topics: beliefs, routines, substance use, trust, jealousy, conflict, shared responsibilities.

Two stories, one shared cycle

Strengths we bring

02 · NAME WHAT MATTERS

Values into everyday actions

Choose guiding values, then describe what someone could see or hear when you live them.

Mark values that matter to you, then choose their importance. Understanding matters more than matching.

03 · SPEAK SO YOU CAN BE HEARD

Take turns: speaker & listener

Keep the conversation specific, grounded in the present, and open to both perspectives. One topic at a time.

Speaker: make it personal and clear

Listener: understand before responding

If tension rises, pause and agree when you will return. A clear, respectful pause is different from using silence to punish or shut down the conversation.
04 · BE A TEAM UNDER PRESSURE

A three-part support check-in

Start with a manageable stressor outside the relationship. Take turns sharing and supporting, then switch roles.

1 · Explore (15–30 min)

Share what happened, what you thought and felt, and why it touched a tender spot. The listener attends, summarizes, and asks open questions.

2 · Support (about 10 min)

Offer empathy first. Ask before giving advice or practical help.

3 · Feedback (about 5 min)

Say what felt helpful and what else could help. Then switch roles.

Notice the kind of stress

Stress can come from within or outside the relationship; it may be sudden or ongoing, major or part of daily life. Responses can reduce strain or add to it.

Helpful responses can include emotional or practical support, joint problem-solving, sharing information, or taking on a task. Avoid sarcasm, blame, and criticism disguised as help.

05 · SMALL MOMENTS ADD UP

Care & companionship planner

Think of specific, low-cost actions and activities that would feel meaningful. Choose what is manageable and welcome.

Small caring behaviors

Things we enjoy doing together

Choose simple activities that fit your time and energy. A short walk, shared meal, music, or unrushed conversation counts.

This week: gentle tracker

Check a day after a caring action or shared activity. This is an invitation, not a scorecard.

06 · FROM STUCK TO SPECIFIC

Problem-solving, side by side

Take turns answering. Aim for a plan both people can try, then review what happened.

Understand the problem

Start gently: say something positive, be specific, name feelings, acknowledge your role, and check your understanding.

Generate options

Focus on solutions first. Brainstorm freely, involve both people, and stay open to influence.

Make a trial plan

Agree on a time to review. The plan should not leave either person resentful; solve the problem, not the person.

Review & repair

A setback is information. Lower the stakes, return to calm, and decide together what to try next.

07 · CLOSENESS AT YOUR OWN PACE

Curiosity, comfort & consent

Talk about what helps each person feel close. There is no required pace or destination. Either person can say no, pause, or change their mind.

What helps me feel connected?

Take turns talking about emotional closeness, pleasurable touch, activities, setting, and imagination. Share preferences and boundaries without pressure.

A flexible closeness spectrum

Some couples find it useful to discuss levels of physical intimacy without assuming one should lead to another.

1 · Affection

Holding hands, hugging, kissing, or other wanted affectionate touch.

2 · Sensual touch

Cuddling or non-genital touch, with or without clothing, if both want it.

3 · Playful touch

Explore affectionate or sensual touch together, checking in and respecting limits.

4 · Erotic touch

Any mutually wanted erotic touch. Consent and comfort stay active throughout.

5 · Intercourse or another shared choice

Only if both freely want this. Intimacy does not need to follow a sequence.

When you hear “no,” hesitation, or a change of mind, stop, listen, and ask what would feel safe now.
